What is a DX Cluster?

A practical guide for radio amateurs

{% endblock %} {% block filters %} {% endblock %} {% block contents %}

A DX cluster is a network of nodes where radio amateurs share DX spots in real time: short reports of distant stations that were just heard or worked. Instead of tuning across the whole band, you read the list and go straight to the frequency where a rare country is on the air. This site shows the spots collected by our DXSpider node in a live web page, with filters for band, mode and text, and it is free to use.

What is a DX spot?

A spot is a single line sent by an operator: the frequency, the callsign of the DX station, the callsign of the spotter, the UTC time and a free comment, usually the mode (CW, SSB, FT8, RTTY) and the signal report. The cluster adds the country of the station, taken from its callsign prefix.

How to read the spots table

  • DE: the spotter, the station that reported the signal.
  • Freq: the frequency in kHz, coloured by band (160 m to 6 m, VHF and above).
  • Mode: CW, SSB, FT8, FT4, RTTY and other modes, guessed from the comment or from the frequency. Click it to show only that mode.
  • DX: the station that was heard. The radio-waves icon opens a WebSDR already tuned on the frequency, so you can check whether the signal reaches you too.
  • Country with its flag, Comments and UTC time.

New spots are highlighted and, if you want, announced by a soft beep that you can switch off from the speaker icon. The quick filter bar lets you pick the bands and modes you care about; the search box finds a callsign, a country or a word in the comment.

Bands and modes to watch

On HF the best band changes during the day: 40, 30 and 20 metres are usually open in the afternoon and evening, 15, 12 and 10 metres open around midday when the solar activity is good, 80 and 160 metres are the night bands. Digital modes concentrate on fixed frequencies; the common FT8 dial frequencies are 1.840, 3.573, 7.074, 10.136, 14.074, 18.100, 21.074, 24.915, 28.074 and 50.313 MHz. CW sits at the bottom of each band and SSB above it. The propagation page shows solar flux and MUF to help you choose, and the statistics page shows which bands have the most spots by hour of the day.

How to connect by telnet

You can also use the cluster directly from a logging program or a terminal. Connect with a telnet client to cluster.iu3cyv.eu on port 7300 and log in with your callsign:

telnet cluster.iu3cyv.eu 7300

Useful DXSpider commands: sh/dx shows the latest spots, sh/dx on 20m filters a band, dx 14074 CALLSIGN FT8 announces a spot of your own.

DXpeditions

Stations from rare entities are marked with a globe icon in the spots table when an announced DX operation is known for that callsign (ADXO data from NG3K). Follow them early: DXpeditions are on the air for a limited time and the pile-ups are largest in the first days.

Frequently asked questions

Is it free?

Yes, both the web DX cluster and the telnet access are free for radio amateurs.

Do I need an account?

No account is needed to read the spots. To send spots by telnet you log in with your callsign.

Which software is behind it?

The node runs DXSpider and the web interface is based on Spiderweb by IU1BOW, with the changes of this fork.
